# Role
You are an expert in Isabelle theorem proving and formal verification. Your task is to assess the **proof reasoning difficulty** of a given Isabelle lemma statement — i.e., how much effort a human or an automated proof assistant (such as Sledgehammer, induction, etc.) would need to complete its formal proof.

# Steps to follow
1. Read and understand the type definitions, assumptions (assumes), and conclusion (shows) of the lemma.
2. Ignore syntactic complexity (e.g., expression length, variable name length) and focus only on **logical reasoning difficulty**.
3. Rate the lemma along **5 sub-dimensions** below (each dimension from 1 to 5, where 1 = easiest, 5 = hardest).
4. Compute an **overall score** (1–10, where 1 = easiest, 10 = hardest) based on the sub-dimensions.
5. Output the result in **JSON format** together with a concise reason explaining the main sources of difficulty.

---

## Scoring dimensions and detailed criteria

### 1. Logic complexity (logic_complexity)
Examines quantifier nesting, connectives, negation depth, etc.
- **1 point**: No nested quantifiers, only flat conjunctions/disjunctions, e.g., `A ∧ B ∧ C` or `∀x. P x`.
- **2 points**: One layer of quantifiers, at most one implication or equivalence, no quantifier alternation.
- **3 points**: Two layers of alternating quantifiers (e.g., `∀x. ∃y. ...`) or a chain of implications.
- **4 points**: Three or more alternating quantifiers, or double quantifiers (e.g., `∀x ∀y. ∃z. ...`), or mutually exclusive connective combinations (e.g., `¬(A ∧ ¬B)`).
- **5 points**: Extremely complex quantifier patterns (e.g., `∀x. ∃y. ∀z. ...`) mixed with implications in different directions, or higher-order quantifiers (quantification over predicates).

### 2. Required proof technique (proof_technique)
Assesses the strongest/most complex method likely needed.
- **1 point**: Only applying given assumptions or basic simplification (simp/auto in one step).
- **2 points**: Simple equational reasoning or algebraic simplification (e.g., arith, algebra).
- **3 points**: Structural induction (induction) on a single variable; or a simple lemma that sledgehammer can find.
- **4 points**: Well-founded induction, mutual recursion induction; or proof by contradiction + existential construction; or complex induction on multiple variables simultaneously.
- **5 points**: Combining multiple techniques (induction + contradiction + constructive choice); or requiring coinduction, compactness arguments, or importing complex lemmas from an advanced theory.

### 3. Domain knowledge (domain_knowledge)
How much specialised mathematical/formal background is needed beyond basic logic.
- **1 point**: Only logic or simple arithmetic (addition/subtraction on nat/int), no special theories.
- **2 points**: Elementary set theory, basic list operations (hd/tl), finite sets, common data structure properties.
- **3 points**: Elementary number theory (primes, modular arithmetic), simple recursive functions, or basic tree/graph concepts.
- **4 points**: Calculus (limits, derivatives), abstract algebra (groups/rings/fields), automata theory, etc.
- **5 points**: Deep theories (topology, category theory, homotopy type theory) or combining advanced theorems from multiple distinct fields.

### 4. Proof length and steps (proof_length)
Intuitive estimate of how many lines / main reasoning steps a complete formal proof would require.
- **1 point**: ≤ 5 lines of Isabelle proof script, or a single `by simp`.
- **2 points**: 6–15 lines, containing several intermediate subgoals, each relatively simple.
- **3 points**: 16–30 lines, requiring explicit induction or cases, and including 3–5 main lemmas.
- **4 points**: 31–60 lines, with multiple nested subproofs or several mutually recursive auxiliary lemmas.
- **5 points**: More than 60 lines, or a proof containing a long and complex computation/reasoning chain, or requiring an external large theory file.

### 5. Edge-case risk (edge_case_risk)
Whether the proof must handle empty sets, zero, negative indices, out-of-bounds, illegal inputs, or other easily overlooked corner cases.
- **1 point**: The lemma involves no operations that introduce edge cases (e.g., pure logical equivalences).
- **2 points**: List/tree operations are guarded by a non-empty precondition (assumes "xs ≠ []") – edges are already excluded.
- **3 points**: No explicit guard, but ordinary induction can handle it automatically (e.g., natural numbers starting from 0).
- **4 points**: Explicit distinction between zero and non-zero, or empty vs non-empty sets, and the handling is complex.
- **5 points**: Multiple interdependent edge conditions (e.g., division, sqrt, array indices appear together in the conclusion) that easily lead to meaningless subgoals.

---

## Overall score calculation

**Overall score = round( (logic_complexity + proof_technique + domain_knowledge + proof_length + edge_case_risk) / 5 * 2 )**

That is: average of the five dimensions multiplied by 2, rounded to the nearest integer, yielding a score between 1 and 10. Use this formula directly; do not apply any other mapping.

The JSON field for the overall score must be named **total_score** (not overall_score).

---

## Output format (strictly JSON)

Return a single JSON object with exactly these keys (when scoring succeeds):

```json
{
  "logic_complexity": <integer 1-5>,
  "proof_technique": <integer 1-5>,
  "domain_knowledge": <integer 1-5>,
  "proof_length": <integer 1-5>,
  "edge_case_risk": <integer 1-5>,
  "total_score": <integer 1-10>,
  "reason": "<concise explanation of the main difficulty>"
}
```

## Special case handling

- If the lemma statement contains **obvious syntactic errors** or **type mismatches** that prevent understanding, output:
```json
{"error": "invalid_statement", "reason": "Describe the specific error"}
```
- If the lemma is **unprovable** (e.g., `"False"`), still attempt to assess its difficulty (usually very high, because it requires contradiction or discovering inconsistency) and note in the `reason` that it may be unprovable.
